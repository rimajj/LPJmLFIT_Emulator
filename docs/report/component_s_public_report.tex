\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Component S is the slow, annual component of a hybrid land model for use inside an Earth System Model. Once per simulated year it replaces an explicit, individual-tree demography calculation with a trained statistical prediction: given a summary of how a patch of forest fared physically that year, it returns how many trees are alive and what functional traits the newly established ones carry. It is trained on the output of LPJmL-FIT, an individual- and trait-based dynamic global vegetation model, and validated out-of-sample against it.

\textbf{Current status.} Component S reproduces LPJmL-FIT's forest \emph{structure} globally at close to the achievable accuracy limit: tree density at $R^2 = 0.982$ per patch-year and $0.999$ per grid cell, and all four functional-trait distributions with pooled Kolmogorov--Smirnov distances below $0.012$. It runs on the complete seven tree plant-functional-type set, over both a historical and an SSP3-7.0 future-climate period, on 58{,}588 grid cells. Serialised artifacts are dependency-free and load in seconds.

\textbf{Principal open items, in order of seriousness.} The first is structural rather than statistical. Inside a live coupled run -- where the component is called each year on its own previous output -- the emulated stand's tree density is advanced by predicted year-on-year \emph{ratios} and is never anchored to an absolute level, so an error in how many trees there are is never corrected. Measured over ten coupled years in five contrasting locations, three of the five end 36--81\,\% above the reference model; measured over 300 years of identical climate, a fourfold error in initial density is still fourfold at the end. The second is that reproducing the \emph{level} of a quantity is not the same as reproducing its \emph{change} under warming, and Component S understates the warming-driven shift in wood density by about 40 percent. Component S is therefore a validated structural emulator, a not-yet-validated response emulator, and not yet stable under multi-decadal recursive use. Sections \ref{sec:standing} and \ref{sec:roadmap} state precisely what this permits today and what closing it requires.

This report is written for ecologists, geoscientists and vegetation modellers. Plain-language explanations of each statistical measure are given in Section \ref{sec:measures}; complete specifications of inputs, targets and model dimensions are tabulated in Sections \ref{sec:data} and \ref{sec:method} for readers who need to reproduce or couple to the component.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose and design}

An Earth System Model needs the land surface to supply water, energy and carbon fluxes every timestep. Vegetation controls those fluxes, and vegetation itself changes on much longer timescales -- trees establish, compete, and die over decades to centuries. Resolving that demography explicitly, tree by tree, is what makes a model like LPJmL-FIT scientifically detailed and simultaneously far too slow to run repeatedly inside a coupled climate simulation at global scale.

The hybrid land model this work belongs to splits the problem by timescale:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{F} -- a fast, differentiable, conserving daily biophysical core (photosynthesis, water and carbon fluxes), retained from LPJmL-FIT and reimplemented to be automatically differentiable.
\item \textbf{E} -- a surface-energy-balance and skin-temperature closure, which LPJmL-FIT lacks and a coupled model requires.
\item \textbf{S} -- \emph{this component}: the slow, annual demography and trait step, replaced by a trained statistical emulator.
\end{itemize}

Component S is called once per simulated year. It receives that year's physical summary from F and returns an updated forest: a tree count, and traits for new recruits. It answers two questions, and is built from two correspondingly different statistical models (Section \ref{sec:method}):

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{How many trees are now alive in this patch?} A count, predicted by a distributional random forest.
\item \textbf{What traits do the new trees have?} A jointly consistent bundle of four functional traits per recruit, drawn from a conditional Gaussian copula.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} Component S covers \emph{trees}. Grasses in the same patch are handled by component F and are not discussed further. Traits are assigned to \emph{recruits}; mortality in the deployed configuration is trait-blind, which is why the target for the trait model is LPJmL-FIT's \emph{survivor} trait distribution rather than its establishment distribution. A trait-dependent mortality operator has since been built and measured (Section \ref{sec:traitmort}); it is off by default and is not part of the deployed artifact, so every result in this report is on the trait-blind configuration.

\subsection{Prediction targets}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Count model:} \texttt{n\_living}, the number of living trees in a patch at year end (observed range 1--48).
\item \textbf{Trait model, production:} the joint distribution of \texttt{SLA}, \texttt{Wooddens}, \texttt{D95max}, \texttt{minwscal} over recruits. These four are exactly the traits LPJmL-FIT samples at establishment, and they map onto the carbon pools the coupled model requires.
\item \textbf{Trait model, diagnostic:} \texttt{agb} and \texttt{Height} per stem, validated identically but \emph{never} written into the production artifact, so an error in them cannot propagate into a coupled run.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method and model dimensions}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Counts: a distributional random forest}

A single decision tree splits the input space by asking successive threshold questions (``is growth efficiency above this value?''), ending in a leaf holding the training rows that satisfied all of them. A random forest grows many such trees, each on a different random subsample of rows and considering a random subset of predictors at each split, and combines them. The randomisation is what prevents the ensemble from memorising individual rows.

\emph{Distributional} means each leaf retains the full set of training values that reached it, not only their mean. A prediction is therefore a weighted empirical distribution over outcomes, from which a mean, a quantile, or a random draw can be taken. This matters here because forest demography is genuinely stochastic: the useful answer is ``how many trees are plausible in this situation,'' not only the single most likely number.

\subsection{Traits: a conditional Gaussian copula}

The four traits are correlated -- dense wood tends to accompany particular leaf economics -- so drawing them independently would generate combinations that never occur in nature or in LPJmL-FIT. A copula separates the problem into the \emph{shape} of each trait's own distribution and the \emph{dependence} between traits:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item Draw four correlated standard normal values, $z \sim N(0, I)$, and correlate them as $z_c = L z$, where $L$ is the Cholesky factor of the trait correlation matrix $R = L L^{\mathsf{T}}$.
\item Convert each to a uniform number on $[0,1]$ via the normal cumulative distribution function, $u = \Phi(z_c)$. These four uniforms carry the dependence but no marginal shape.
\item Map each uniform through the corresponding trait's own conditional distribution -- supplied by a per-trait distributional random forest conditioned on the 8 environmental predictors of Table \ref{tab:trait_inputs} -- to obtain the trait value.
\end{enumerate}

The result is a recruit whose four traits are individually correctly distributed for its environment \emph{and} jointly consistent with one another.

\section{Validation design}
\label{sec:validation}

Four independent checks are applied. Each answers a different question, and they are reported separately rather than merged into one score.

\paragraph{1. Held out by location.} Every grid cell is assigned to one of five groups. Each group is set aside in turn, a fresh model is trained on the remaining cells only, and that model predicts the withheld group. Every cell in the data set is therefore predicted exactly once by a model that never saw it. This is the basis for all headline numbers in Section \ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{2. Held out by contiguous region (blocked).} Groups assigned by cell index are spatially scattered, so a withheld cell usually has trained neighbours 50\,km away in essentially the same climate -- and part of the apparent skill is then spatial interpolation rather than learned environmental response. The blocked design withholds entire $15^\circ \times 5^\circ$ blocks so that a cell's neighbours are withheld with it. Comparing the two designs separates real response from interpolation, and the result is reported in Section \ref{sec:standing}.

\paragraph{3. Held out by climate period.} The model is trained on the historical period alone and evaluated on the future-climate period it has never seen, and vice versa. This tests transfer to a different climate regime rather than to a different place.

\paragraph{4. Against the reference model's own repeatability -- the noise floor.}
\label{sec:floor}
LPJmL-FIT is stochastic: rerunning identical climate at an identical location with a different random seed yields a different, equally valid forest. No predictor that sees climate rather than the random-number stream can match either run exactly. That limit is measurable as the agreement between two seeds, and it is the correct standard against which to judge the emulator -- an emulator at the noise floor cannot be improved on, whatever its raw score.

The comparison is made attenuation-aware. Two reliabilities are estimated: the emulator's self-consistency (splitting its predictions at each location into interleaved halves and correlating the half-medians, then applying the standard correction for having used half the data) and the reference model's seed-to-seed reliability. The attainable \textbf{ceiling} is the geometric mean of the two, $\sqrt{\rho_P \rho_Y}$ -- looking through two imperfect windows is blurrier than either alone but not the sum of both blurs. The emulator's score divided by that ceiling is reported as $r_\mathrm{center}$; a value near 1 means the remaining error is irreducible rather than a modelling deficiency.

\section{Statistical measures used in this report}
\label{sec:measures}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.35em]
\item[$R^2$ (coefficient of determination)] The fraction of the variation in the true values that the prediction accounts for. $1.0$ is perfect; $0$ is no better than always predicting the overall mean. $R^2 = 0.98$ means 98\,\% of the variation is captured.
\item[RMSE (root-mean-square error)] The typical size of an individual miss, in the same units as the quantity itself, with large misses weighted more heavily than small ones. An RMSE of $0.69$ trees means a typical patch-level count error below one tree.
\item[Correlation ($r$, Pearson; $\rho$, Spearman)] How well predicted and true values move together. Pearson uses the values; Spearman uses only their rank order and so is insensitive to a monotonic distortion of scale. Reporting both distinguishes ``wrong ordering'' from ``right ordering, wrong scale''.
\item[Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance (KS)] The largest gap between two cumulative distributions -- a whole-shape comparison rather than a comparison of averages. $0$ is identical; $1$ is disjoint. Values below $\sim$0.02 indicate distributions that are difficult to distinguish. KS computed at a single grid cell is inherently larger than KS pooled globally, because one cell holds far fewer trees; the two must not be compared with each other.
\item[nqrmse] RMSE divided by the quantity's own interquartile range, so that traits in different units become comparable. It is unsuitable for strongly heavy-tailed quantities such as biomass, where KS should be read instead.
\item[Prediction-spread ratio] The standard deviation of the predictions divided by that of the truth. $1.0$ means the model reproduces the real geographic spread of a trait; below $1.0$ means it hedges toward the middle and under-represents genuine extremes.
\item[$r_\mathrm{center}$] Skill relative to the attainable ceiling of Section \ref{sec:floor}, rather than to a hypothetical perfect prediction.
\end{description}

\section{Where Component S stands}

\subsection{Established capability}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Global forest structure, at or near the attainable ceiling.} Tree density at $R^2 = 0.982$ per patch-year and $0.999$ per cell; all four trait distributions matching in global shape (pooled KS $\le 0.012$) and at most individual locations; stand biomass consistent end-to-end at $R^2 = 0.931$.
\item \textbf{Transfer to an unseen climate regime.} Held-out-by-period accuracy matches held-out-by-location accuracy for counts, and trait fidelity is equal or better under SSP3-7.0 than historically.
\item \textbf{Complete PFT coverage} -- all seven tree types, 58{,}588 cells, both scenarios.
\item \textbf{A verified stochastic reference.} Two genuinely independent seeds exist for both scenarios, so the noise floor and hence the attainable ceiling are measured rather than assumed.
\item \textbf{Deployable artifacts.} Dependency-free serialised models (52\,MB counts, 129\,MB traits) that load in seconds and carry their own input-order contract, so a mismatch with the calling model is rejected at construction rather than producing silently wrong recruits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The skill is environmental response, not spatial lookup}

Because the standard hold-out scatters withheld cells among trained neighbours, part of the apparent skill could in principle be spatial interpolation. Testing this directly with contiguous blocked hold-out (design 2 of Section \ref{sec:validation}):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item A deliberately constructed \emph{position-only} predictor -- given latitude and longitude and nothing else -- reproduces $r = 0.837$ for wood density under scattered hold-out but collapses to $0.14$--$0.21$ when whole contiguous regions are withheld. A predictor that knows only ``where am I'' cannot understand forests, yet under the scattered design it appeared competitive. This calibrates how much of a scattered-fold score can be interpolation. Both figures come from a deliberately weak nearest-neighbour screen, run in advance of the forests as a conservative measure of how much information a predictor set \emph{contains} rather than as a forecast of what a forest will score with it; on that same screen the emulator's own predictors give $0.799$ scattered against $0.607$ and $0.628$ under two independent block layouts.
\item Tested as full forests, the \emph{deployed} predictors keep most of their skill when adjacency is severed: per-cell wood-density $r = 0.869$ under scattered hold-out becomes $0.734$ and $0.748$ under two independent $15^\circ \times 5^\circ$ block layouts. Most of what Component S knows is therefore transferable environmental response. A retained \emph{percentage} is deliberately not quoted: measured on the six-descriptor moisture variant that ratio is $0.78 \pm 0.41$, and it reads $0.53$ or $0.37$ depending on whether ranks or transformed correlations are used, so it is not a stable quantity. The direction is the reportable result.
\end{itemize}

Consequently the headline numbers of Section \ref{sec:results} should be read as accuracy at a location whose neighbours are known -- which is the operationally relevant quantity, since the component runs on a fixed global grid where neighbours always exist -- and not as extrapolation into an unsurveyed region.

\subsection{What a live coupled run does, measured in five contrasting cells}
\label{sec:coupled}

That is not how the component is used. Inside a coupled run it is called year after year on \emph{its own} previous output, and only a closed-loop run can show what that does.

That measurement now exists, in the multi-cell coupled configuration that consumes the artifacts of Table \ref{tab:dims}: ten coupled years of historical climate (2010--2019) in five contrasting cells -- boreal Siberia, temperate Germany, Mediterranean Iberia, the semi-arid Sahel and Amazonian rain forest.

\subsection{The dominant limitation: the coupled stand's density has no level anchor}
\label{sec:anchor}

Section \ref{sec:coupled} left roughly a third of the coupled count error unaccounted for by the loop's compounding. That remainder has been traced, and its cause is structural rather than statistical.

The count model predicts an \emph{absolute} number of trees, and predicts it well. The coupled loop, however, consumes only the year-on-year \emph{ratio} of successive predictions, and advances the stand multiplicatively: this year's tree density is last year's density times that ratio. The convenience is real -- a ratio is unit-free, so a per-patch tree count can drive a stand carried as a density without either side needing to know the area of a patch -- but the price is that the absolute level is never stated anywhere in the loop. The stand's density is its initial value multiplied by a product of ratios, and nothing in the system says what that density ought to be.

That has a directly testable consequence: a level error, once made, should never be corrected. It was tested by perturbing \emph{only} the initial stand density -- same climate, same random seed, same artifacts, everything else identical -- and running for 300 years.

Three consequences follow, and the first is what makes this a finding rather than a restatement.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{The internal prediction converges to a common attractor; the stand it carries does not.} Varying the initial tree count the emulator is seeded with from 7 to 15 -- a 73\,\% spread -- leaves its own internal predicted count almost fully converged after 150 years, with a terminal spread of 6.7\,\% and four of the five runs landing on the same value, while the physical stand those same runs carry still retains 60.2\,\% of its spread. The prediction has a restoring force; the stand does not, because the prediction's \emph{level} never reaches it. This dissociation is also why the defect went unnoticed for so long: a diagnostic watching only the predicted count reports a self-correcting system, and did.
\item \textbf{A training-side mismatch compounds on top of this (``exposure bias'').} At training time the previous tree count -- predictor 11 of Table \ref{tab:count_inputs} -- is always the reference model's own true count for the year before. In a coupled run it is the emulator's own previous output, errors included. The model is therefore asked to predict from inputs of a kind it never saw while training, and that is the mechanism behind the compounding 2.6--4.9\,\% per year of Section \ref{sec:coupled}. It is a \emph{separate} defect from the missing anchor, with a separate fix -- either training the model on its own predictions or dropping the previous count from the predictor set -- and either one requires retraining the global model. Neither fix substitutes for the other: an anchor stops a level error persisting, retraining stops the year-on-year bias that generates one, and the level would remain unanchored however accurate the single-step prediction became.
\item \textbf{The initial tree density is therefore a free parameter of every coupled answer, not an initialisation detail.} Because a level difference is retained indefinitely, whatever density a cell is started at is carried for the length of the run. The deployed pooled artifact does not itself supply a per-cell initial count, and the two legitimate values available for the test location -- 11.0 and 7.0 trees per patch, inherited from the two periods the pooled model was trained on -- are far enough apart to change a measured outcome there by several times the whole size of the reference model's warming shift in wood density (Section \ref{sec:traitmort}). Any coupled result should therefore state the initial density it was started from, and that choice deserves the scrutiny given to a parameter, because in the absence of a restoring force it is one.
\end{itemize}

The absolute density of a coupled stand is thus set by its initialisation and by the accumulated history of every imperfect year, and by nothing else. That is entirely compatible with the single-step accuracy of Section \ref{sec:results} -- the count model's absolute skill is real, it is simply discarded at the interface -- and it is why multi-decadal or online use needs the interface change of Section \ref{sec:roadmap}, item 1, rather than more predictive skill.

\subsection{The second limitation: warming response is damped}
\label{sec:damping}

Reproducing a quantity's \emph{level} and reproducing its \emph{change} are different problems, and Component S is presently much better at the first.

The reason they differ is one of magnitude. The spatial contrast between a cold cell and a hot cell is large; the change at a single cell between the historical period and 2100 is small -- measured at only 20--31\,\% of the spatial signal. A correlation computed across all locations is therefore dominated by spatial contrast and is roughly three to five times less sensitive to the warming response. A high $R^2$ can coexist with a poorly reproduced response, and will barely register it.

Isolating the response gives:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{The warming-driven shift in wood density is understated by $39.9$\,\%.} LPJmL-FIT shows a mean shift of $+2433$ (internal units); Component S predicts the same direction but $971.5$ units less, i.e.\ $+1461$. The direction is right; the magnitude is not.
\item \textbf{The spatial pattern of the change is only partly captured.} For wood density -- the adjudicating axis -- the pattern correlation is $0.375$ against a self-consistency ceiling of $0.954$, i.e.\ $39$\,\% of what is attainable. Ceilings for the four axes lie between $0.94$ and $0.97$. The pattern is therefore genuinely present in the data and learnable, not noise. \emph{Basis note:} these ceilings are computed on the patch-year reliability split; an earlier stem-parity split gave systematically lower ceilings ($0.87$--$0.96$) and correspondingly flattering percentages, and is superseded.
\item \textbf{The defect is placement, not shrinkage}, and this narrows the search for a fix. The amplitude of the predicted shift is nearly perfect -- a dispersion ratio of $1.034$ against a target of $1$ -- while its pattern captures only $39$\,\% of ceiling. Component S produces right-sized shifts in the wrong cells, which then cancel in the global mean. Pure shrinkage would damp the mean and the dispersion together, and does not. Any lever justified by ``fixing attenuation'' is therefore aimed at a defect that is not present.
\item The damping is a property of the current approach rather than of any optional extra input: the deployed 8-predictor configuration damps by 39.9\,\% on its own.
\end{itemize}

One methodological consequence is worth stating for anyone proposing a fix: reducing the damping is \emph{not} by itself evidence of improvement. A zero-information control model exhibits the least damping of any variant tested while having the worst response pattern. Any candidate must therefore be demonstrated on the response \emph{pattern} as well as its magnitude, under both hold-out designs.

\subsection{An optional trait-dependent mortality operator: the right level change, no measurable response change}
\label{sec:traitmort}

The damping of Section \ref{sec:damping} has an identified mechanistic cause, and the natural fix for it has now been built, wired in and measured. This subsection reports that work because its outcome is instructive and partly negative.

In LPJmL-FIT, the warming-driven wood-density shift is not mainly a change in which species are present. Decomposed, it is $22$\,\% species composition, $51$\,\% a shift \emph{within} each functional type, and the remainder interaction; and the within-type part is a within-age-class effect, i.e.\ differential \emph{survival} of individuals that already differ in wood density. The deployed emulator has no channel for this at all: it thins every cohort by one common factor, which preserves the trait composition exactly. Whatever else it does, it cannot produce a within-type selection signal.

LPJmL-FIT's per-individual mortality hazard was therefore ported directly from the reference model's source -- with its genuinely per-functional-type parameters generated from that source into a checked table, since they differ substantially between types -- and wired in as an \emph{optional} replacement for uniform thinning. The count model still sets how many trees die; the hazard sets \emph{which} ones, through a proportional-hazards tilt that reproduces the reference model's own hazard exactly in the limit where the two agree. The operator is off by default, and with it off every path, baseline and gradient check in this report is unchanged.

Measured at a single location (Hainich) over $81$ years, against a matched control re-run in the same process, and replicated over $12$ random seeds:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{It changes the \emph{level} of community wood density substantially, robustly, and in the right direction}: $+7041 \pm 334$ internal units on the deployed artifact, a $t$-statistic of $21$. That is roughly three times the size of the reference model's warming shift, so the mechanism is not weak.
\item \textbf{It reproduces the reference model's structural signature}: mean wood density rising with cohort age, which is the age-resolved fingerprint of differential survival and is the acceptance target this operator was built against.
\item \textbf{Its contribution to the warming \emph{response} is not distinguishable from zero}: $+0.26$ times the reference shift, with a $95$\,\% interval of $[-0.38, +0.90]$. The operator fixes a level, not a response.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What bounds the operator is the count channel, not the hazard.} The operator does not decide how many trees die -- the count model does -- it only chooses \emph{which} of them die, out of the number the count model demands. In the reference model deaths and establishment happen together every year, so a stand whose tree number barely changes is nonetheless replacing a few per cent of its stems annually, and it is that gross turnover that selection acts on. The emulator's annual step is net-only: within one year it either thins or establishes, never both, so its gross turnover \emph{is} its net change, and a year in which the predicted count does not move is a year without selection. Measured at the same single location under real year-to-year climate, the ported hazard would remove about $1.4$\,\% of stems per year while the count model's demanded net change is $1.9$\,\% historically and $2.2$\,\% under SSP3-7.0, so the operator gets to act at at least half the reference model's own hazard strength in $54$\,\% of thinning years historically and $62$\,\% under warming -- warming loosens the constraint rather than tightening it. Under an artificially \emph{constant} climate the same statistic is only $14$\,\%, because a forest's prediction is piecewise constant and a repeated year barely moves it; that figure is a property of the test forcing rather than of the emulator, and the two above supersede it. Allowing a single year to apply the hazard's gross deaths \emph{and} a compensating influx of recruits is the identified next step for this operator; it changes the count identity and the recruit channel at the same time, so it is its own experiment with its own control.

Two cautions govern how far this can be read. It is one location, and the report's other results are global. And a single-location $81$-year experiment of this kind is intrinsically noisy: the seed-to-seed spread of the response statistic is about the same size as the effect being measured, so roughly $115$ seeds would be needed to resolve an effect of the size actually observed. An earlier single-seed run of exactly this experiment reported a large positive response contribution; replication withdrew it. The level result survives replication and is the quotable one.

A third caution is procedural, and it is the reason the numbers above are quotable at all. A measurement with this operator is interpretable only if two preconditions hold in every year of the run: the hazard killed no tree outright, and it never overrode the count model's own target. Changing only the initial tree count at the test location from 11.0 to 7.0 trees per patch violates both, and the measured contribution to the warming response then swings from $+0.76$ to $-3.71$ times the reference shift -- so a run that violates them is discarded rather than averaged in. The quoted figures also required raising the cap on how many distinct tree cohorts the emulator carries: at the shipped default the merging of similar cohorts never fires under constant climate but wakes eight or nine times per 81-year run under realistic interannual climate, and when it is forced to fire it distorts the community trait mean by three to five times the size of the signal being measured. The shipped cohort cap is therefore unsuitable for a transient run, and ``the merge is dormant'' is a property of a forcing configuration rather than of the cap.

\section{Remaining work, and how the objective is reached}
\label{sec:roadmap}

The objective is a land component that runs online inside an Earth System Model, reproducing LPJmL-FIT's forest dynamics faithfully at a small fraction of its cost. Six steps remain, in priority order.

\paragraph{1. Give the recursive stand a level anchor.}
This is now the first priority, ahead of the warming-response gap, because it compounds without bound and because no amount of predictive skill fixes it. Section \ref{sec:anchor} measures the defect; Section \ref{sec:coupled} shows what it costs over ten coupled years.

The fix is specified: carry the count-to-density conversion across the component interface and blend the multiplicative update toward the absolute target with a stated relaxation time, rather than replacing it outright. A hard anchor would be the wrong shape, since it would discard the physical core's own stand dynamics and convert a level drift into a level bias. The tempting local shortcut -- anchoring to the previous predicted \emph{count} instead, without touching the interface -- is not available either: a per-patch count and a stand density differ by exactly the unknown patch area, so using one as the other silently sets that constant to 1 and would look anchored while being biased. It is therefore not a local change -- it modifies an interface contract between two components and moves every coupled baseline -- which is why it is scoped rather than done, and when it is done it must be scored with the reference count fed back as well as freely running, or the ratio and level effects are confounded again.

\paragraph{2. Close the warming-response gap (Section \ref{sec:damping}).}
A candidate exists and has been measured. Widening the trait model's predictors from 8 to 14 by adding the six moisture descriptors of Table \ref{tab:trait_inputs}, together with quantile-weighted forests and greater capacity, meets the project's formal trait criteria for the first time -- wood-density skill 0.901 and 58\,\% of the gap to the ceiling closed, with spread ratio rising to 0.854 and distributional fidelity improving on all four axes.

It has nevertheless \emph{not} been promoted to production, for a specific and instructive reason: those six descriptors are present-day climatological means, constant within each cell, so they cannot by construction encode a warming response -- a cell's historical and future rows are identical on them. Under blocked hold-out the wider set buys $+0.031$ of location-level skill while its apparent benefit for the response \emph{reverses sign}. It improves the quantity already near ceiling and does not improve the quantity that is not.

The intended resolution is therefore a \emph{scenario- and time-resolved} version of those moisture descriptors, recomputed per cell and per year from the underlying climate forcing rather than frozen at present-day means, so that the added information can carry a warming signal. This is specified but not yet built.

\paragraph{3. Retrain the count model on the inputs it will actually see.}
At training time the count model is given the reference model's true previous tree count; in a coupled run it is given its own previous output instead (Section \ref{sec:anchor}). Two fixes exist: train the model on its own predictions, feeding predicted counts back during training in increasing proportion, or drop the previous count from the predictor set altogether and accept a harder single-step problem. Both require retraining the global count model, so the size of the one-step bias is worth measuring from the existing training tables before that cost is committed. This is deliberately kept separate from item 1, because neither item substitutes for the other.

\paragraph{4. Complete the ceiling measurement.}
A per-patch-year count noise floor, matched to what the count model actually predicts, is needed so that count accuracy can be stated relative to the attainable ceiling as trait accuracy already is. The trait ceiling under future climate additionally requires a seed-pair trait table built without the subsampling currently used for tractability, since the subsample is seed-dependent and would otherwise depress the floor and flatter the emulator.

\paragraph{5. Complete the recursive-stability battery.}
Every number in Sections \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:damping} is a single annual step evaluated against recorded outcomes, using the reference model's own drivers; Sections \ref{sec:coupled} and \ref{sec:anchor} are the only closed-loop measurements that exist, and they are five cells over ten years plus one cell over three centuries. Item 1 above identifies and quantifies one specific recursive defect, but a level anchor is a necessary rather than a sufficient condition: the remaining checks -- how strongly one year's stand predicts the next, how fast the stand recovers from a perturbation, and whether the memory observed is internal to the emulator rather than inherited from autocorrelated climate -- are properties of the coupled system and belong to the multi-cell coupled configuration, which consumes the artifacts of Table \ref{tab:dims}. They must be measured both freely running and with the reference count fed back, since an unanchored level itself manufactures autocorrelation and slow recovery and would otherwise be mistaken for genuine internal memory.

\paragraph{6. Extend scope.}
Grass demography is currently handled by component F and could be brought under the same emulation approach. Per-PFT rather than pooled trait structure, and joint trait-correlation validation beyond the marginal and per-cell distributional checks reported here, are natural refinements once the higher-priority items above are settled.

\section{Conclusion}

Component S replaces an explicit individual-tree demography calculation with two trained statistical models -- a distributional random forest for tree number, and a conditional Gaussian copula for correlated recruit traits -- learned from LPJmL-FIT output and validated out-of-sample against it.

It reproduces global forest structure at or close to the limit set by the reference model's own stochasticity, across all seven tree functional types, under both historical and SSP3-7.0 climate, and it does so through genuine environmental response rather than spatial lookup. Its deployable artifacts are compact and dependency-free.

Its recursive behaviour inside a live coupled run is now measured, and it is the larger of its two problems: the stand's density is advanced by predicted ratios and never anchored to a level, so an error in it is permanent. Its warming response is damped by about 40 percent. It covers trees only, and it has no CO$_2$ response of any kind, so it is valid at constant or near-historical CO$_2$. Neither of the two problems is a solved one: the response gap has had its most promising candidate measured and refused, and the recursive defect has been localised to a missing level anchor whose fix is specified but crosses a component interface. On present evidence Component S is ready to supply forest structure to a coupled land model, and not yet ready to be the basis of a quantitative claim about the magnitude of forest change under warming.

\section*{Glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=!,labelwidth=3.4cm,style=nextline]
\item[Above-ground biomass (agb)] Mass of trunk, branches and leaves above the soil, excluding roots.
\item[Copula] A construction that separates the individual distribution of each variable from the dependence structure between them, allowing correlated draws with correct marginals.
\item[Distributional random forest] A random forest whose leaves retain the full set of training values reaching them, so predictions are distributions rather than single numbers.
\item[Emulator] A fast statistical model trained to reproduce the input--output behaviour of a slower process model.
\item[Foliar projective cover (fpc)] The fraction of ground shaded by foliage when viewed from above.
\item[Growing-degree days (GDD5)] Accumulated daily temperature above 5\,$^\circ$C over a year; a measure of thermal growing-season length.
\item[Held-out (out-of-sample)] Evaluated on data withheld from the model being scored during its training.
\item[Leaf area index (LAI)] Leaf area per unit ground area.
\item[Noise floor] The accuracy limit imposed by the reference model's own stochasticity: how well one run predicts an independent rerun differing only in random seed.
\item[Patch] A small fixed-area unit of forest simulated independently; 25 per grid cell here, representing the natural spread of forest states.
\item[Plant functional type (PFT)] A class of plants grouped by shared physiology and structure (e.g.\ boreal needleleaved evergreen) rather than by species.
\item[Specific leaf area (SLA)] Leaf area per unit leaf mass; high values indicate thin, light-capturing leaves.
\item[SSP3-7.0] A high-emissions future scenario used for the future-climate period.
\item[Wood density] Mass of wood per unit volume; trades rapid growth against mechanical strength and longevity.
\end{description}
