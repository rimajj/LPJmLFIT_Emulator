\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Individual-based, trait-based vegetation models resolve how forests of differently adapted trees grow, compete and
die, but they are too expensive to run inside Earth system models. We present LandEmu, a learned emulator of the
dynamic global vegetation model LPJmL-FIT that reproduces both its daily carbon and water exchange and its yearly
forest structure and trait composition, driven by daily weather alone. Profiling the original shows that its cost lies
in the daily per-tree physics and in the ensemble of replicate patches it averages over, so the emulator replaces the
daily step as a whole and predicts the expectation over patches. A five-member ensemble of small neural networks
computes the daily fluxes, with snow, top-metre and deep soil water carried as explicit stores that close the water
balance exactly. Once a year, gradient-boosted tree models predict tree density, biomass per tree, four trait medians,
leaf area, vegetation carbon and plant-type cover as a departure from a per-cell anchor, driven by the trailing
30-year climate, and a second boosted model adds the year-to-year departure of tree density and biomass per tree
driven by the year's weather anomaly. Tested on a climate model and an independent run of LPJmL-FIT withheld from
training, the yearly model's error in dense forests is 1.22 (tree density) and 1.18 (biomass per tree) times that of
a further run of the stochastic original, and 0.59--0.86 times for the trait medians. The error grows by a factor of
1.05 between the 2050s and the 2090s. In the original's drought die-offs, which are threshold kills of individual
trees, the emulator follows 18\,\% of the tree loss. Tested on cells withheld from training, the daily networks' 30-year mean fluxes
are 2.4 (gross primary production) to 5.2 (runoff) times further from the original than a further run is --- about
3--6\,\% per cell for production and evapotranspiration --- with area totals within 3\,\%. For a nitrogen-limited
configuration of LPJmL-FIT with rising CO$_2$, the emulator reproduces 81--97\,\% of the change in vegetation carbon
under two unseen climate models (a second run of the original: 61--89\,\%), but only 72\,\% of the largest
warming-only loss, and it damps the cell-to-cell spread of the response. The emulator costs $7.0\times10^{-3}$
core-seconds per cell-year, about 38 and 1\,000 times less than LPJmL-FIT at 25 and 500 patches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Terrestrial ecosystems take up about a third of anthropogenic CO$_2$ emissions \citep{Friedlingstein2023GCB}, and
the vegetation that does so is not a fixed surface but a population of plants that grow, compete, die and are
replaced. Dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs) represent this population in Earth system models (ESMs), usually
through a few plant functional types with fixed properties. A growing body of work argues that this is not enough:
the size structure and the functional diversity of forests shape how they respond to drought and warming, and models
that resolve individuals, cohorts and traits behave differently from those that do not \citep{Fisher2018,Fisher2020}.
LPJmL-FIT, a version of the LPJmL model with individual trees and variable traits
\citep{Sakschewski2015,Sakschewski2016,Thonicke2020}, is one of these models: each tree carries its own specific leaf
area, wood density, rooting depth and drought threshold, drawn when it establishes, and the forest's trait composition
shifts as trees with poorly suited traits die. Amazon forests with more trait diversity recover better from drought in
this model than forests without it \citep{Sakschewski2016}.

The price of this realism is cost. LPJmL-FIT simulates every tree every day, and because establishment and mortality
are random it averages over many replicate patches per grid cell: 25 in the runs used here, around 500 in published
applications. Its run time grows in proportion to the number of patches, which puts it orders of magnitude beyond what
an ESM can afford for its land surface. Emulation --- replacing the expensive model with a fast statistical or
machine-learned approximation --- is the obvious route.

\paragraph{Emulators of land models.} Statistical emulation of land models began as a tool for calibration and
sensitivity analysis. Gaussian-process and polynomial-chaos surrogates map a model's parameters to a few outputs or to
a cost function, so that the expensive model need not be run for every parameter set
\citep{R00530,R00135,R00157,R00002}; history matching and Bayesian calibration of JULES, ORCHIDEE and TRIFFID work
this way \citep{R00221,R00727,R00167}. Such surrogates answer how a summary of a run depends on parameters, not how the
land state evolves under a changing atmosphere, and they cannot stand in for a land component. Since about 2019 deep
learning has widened what an emulator can be: recurrent, convolutional and generative networks map climate forcing to
multivariate trajectories, and differentiable programming lets learned components be trained inside process models
\citep{Reichstein2019,Shen2023}.

A few emulators now reproduce a land model broadly enough to replace part of it. For ECMWF's land scheme ecLand, LSTM,
gradient-boosted and feed-forward emulators of seven prognostic soil and snow states run 44 to 4800 times faster than
the model \citep{R00067}, and aiLand adds the turbulent heat fluxes, predicts state increments and runs stably for four
years \citep{R00593}. For the demographic model ED, a multi-scale LSTM emulates canopy height, biomass, leaf area,
production and soil respiration over 40-year trajectories \citep{R01133}, and a conditional diffusion model emulates
the same outputs probabilistically \citep{R00514}. A statistical emulator of LPJ-GUESS carbon, forestry and crop
outputs serves integrated assessment \citep{R00661}, forest-carbon emulators of LPJ-GUESS predict grid-cell stocks
\citep{R00430}, PHASE predicts the equilibrium of slow carbon pools of the E3SM land model to shorten spin-up by a
factor of 60 \citep{R01211}, and a physically guided emulator of the MATSIRO land surface model keeps its water budget
\citep{R00129}. Statistical emulators of LPJmL carbon fluxes and crop yields have been built for coupling with
climate and economic models \citep{R01025,R00013}, and a canopy-temperature emulator runs inside LPJmL's crop module
\citep{R00568}. Each of these covers part of a land model: soil water and heat with turbulent fluxes, or carbon and
stand structure, or equilibrium pools. None emulates the size and trait distributions of a forest or the mortality of
individual trees; the closest is a state-transition network for the landscape model iLand \citep{Rammer2019}.

The second route keeps the process model and replaces individual processes with learned components. Fire is the most
common target: machine-learned fire models have replaced the process schemes of the E3SM land model and of JSBACH
\citep{R00089,R00911,R00342,R00278}. Learned stomatal conductance and photosynthetic capacity run inside JSBACH within
ICON-ESM \citep{R00602}, learned snow cover improves a climate model over High Mountain Asia \citep{R00902}, and an
LSTM replaces the groundwater solver of a coupled land--subsurface model \citep{R02497}. Differentiable land models
learn parameters or process functions end to end from observations \citep{R00316,R00407,R00975,R01005,R00383}, and
hybrid water--carbon models couple neural networks with conceptual balance equations
\citep{R00532,R00755}. These hybrids inherit the process model's structure and conservation, and they are so far the
only learned components that run online inside the land component of an ESM --- but they leave most of the
computational cost in place.

\paragraph{What goes wrong.} The same literature shows where emulators fail. Tree ensembles trained to predict one
step ahead drift when run freely at long lead times, while networks trained on their own multi-step rollouts do not
\citep{R00067,R00593}: an emulator that carries its own state must be robust to its own errors, a problem known from
imitation learning \citep{Ross2011}. Agreement in the present climate says little about agreement under a changed
climate: an emulator of the ecosystem model ecosys matched present-day net primary production almost exactly yet
underestimated it by 204\,gC\,m$^{-2}$\,yr$^{-1}$ by 2100 \citep{R00289}, and an LPJ-GUESS emulator trained on stylised
climate perturbations failed to transfer to realistic scenarios \citep{R00430}. Offline skill can collapse once
feedbacks act: the spatial correlation of a machine-learned fire model in the E3SM land model fell from 0.96--0.98
offline to 0.59--0.70 online \citep{R00911,R00342}, and an online deep-learning fire model in JSBACH stayed below its
offline skill \citep{R00278}. An LSTM groundwater solver ran stably online but accumulated error late in the run
\citep{R02497}. Water and carbon budgets are closed reliably only when the architecture enforces them rather than a
loss term encouraging them \citep{R00532,R00755}. Tests under climates outside the training range, long free-running
tests and explicit conservation checks remain the exception rather than the rule.

From this evidence follow the properties an emulator must have to stand in for an ESM's land component: prognostic
state that carries the memory of past forcing; outputs that cover what the atmosphere exchanges with the land;
conservation by construction; stability when run freely and under feedback; credibility under climates outside the
present one; and quantified uncertainty with a software interface a host model can call. No published emulator of a
dynamic vegetation model combines them, and none of the full emulators has been coupled to an ESM.

\paragraph{This paper.} We describe LandEmu, an emulator of LPJmL-FIT designed against these properties. Its
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a complete emulator of an individual-tree, trait-based DGVM (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}) --- daily gross and net
        primary production, interception, transpiration, soil evaporation, runoff, snow and soil water, together with
        yearly tree density, biomass per tree, four trait medians, leaf area, vegetation carbon and plant-type cover
        --- that runs from daily weather alone and conserves water exactly;
  \item a design derived from where the original spends its run time and from what the literature shows to fail,
        with every architectural and training choice backed by a measured comparison (Sects.~\ref{sec:design},
        \ref{sec:choices});
  \item an evaluation that holds out a whole climate model and an independent run of the original (yearly maps) or
        whole blocks of cells (daily networks), and that measures every error against the original's own
        run-to-run variability (Sect.~\ref{sec:eval});
  \item an analysis of the original's drought die-offs and of how far a stand-level emulator can follow them;
  \item a test of whether the emulator learns the warming response when the training climates cover the test
        climate, a full-grid application, and an end-to-end cost measurement on one core against the original at its
        training and publication settings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The model to be emulated: LPJmL-FIT}\label{sec:target}

LPJmL-FIT couples the water, carbon and vegetation dynamics of LPJmL \citep{Schaphoff2018} with an individual-tree
forest module \citep{Sakschewski2015,Thonicke2020}. On each 0.5$^\circ$ grid cell the forest grows on a set of
replicate patches (225\,m$^2$ each). Trees of seven woody plant types establish with traits drawn within type-specific
ranges, or inherited with small variations from large trees of the same patch; they compete for light in a layered
canopy and for water in a 23-layer soil, allocate carbon each year, and die from carbon starvation, age, heat and
drought stress, or are removed when their leaf carbon falls below that of a sapling. Three grass types fill the
remaining space. The daily step computes photosynthesis and stomatal conductance per tree, interception,
transpiration, soil evaporation, snow, infiltration, percolation and runoff; the yearly step allocates carbon, applies
mortality and fire, and establishes new trees. We use the configuration with natural vegetation only. For most of this
paper nitrogen limitation is switched off and atmospheric CO$_2$ is held constant in the future runs (at its 2019
level; 2014 in the runs of Sect.~\ref{sec:global}); this is how LPJmL-FIT is usually run, because without nitrogen
limitation its CO$_2$ fertilisation is unbounded. The emulator of this configuration therefore takes no CO$_2$ input,
which is faithful to the original rather than a gap. Section~\ref{sec:resp} uses a second configuration, with nitrogen
limitation \citep{vonBloh2018} and rising CO$_2$, for which the emulator receives CO$_2$ as an input.

\paragraph{Where the run time goes.} We profiled the unmodified production binary at five contrasting cells
(temperate, tropical, semi-arid, Mediterranean, boreal; 25 patches, one core) and attributed the exclusive run time to
processes, assigning the statically linked mathematical library calls (21--27\,\% of all time) to the routines that
call them (Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}a). Leaf gas exchange takes 36--46\,\% of the run time and soil water another
15--27\,\%. The yearly demography --- allocation, mortality, establishment and turnover --- takes only 0.4--1.1\,\%,
and the daily loop as a whole 97--98\,\%. The marginal cost is 0.20--0.33 core-seconds per cell-year at 25 patches,
and it grows linearly with the number of patches. Even if the eight most expensive processes were free of cost, the
model would speed up at most 30-fold at the temperate cell (Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}b), whereas an
intermediate-resolution atmosphere leaves an allowance of about 0.0135--0.030 core-seconds per land cell-year (10\,\%
of a measured coupled atmosphere's cost at T31 and T63 resolution). Removing the 25-patch ensemble alone is worth a
factor of 25, and of about 500 at the publication setting.

\paragraph{The original is stochastic.} Two independent runs of LPJmL-FIT --- each with its own 1000-year spin-up and
random seed, under identical climate --- disagree on a typical cell by about 6\,\% in the number of trees taller than
5\,m, 12\,\% in biomass per tree, 2--10\,\% in the trait medians and 1.0--1.6\,\% in 30-year mean fluxes. In sparse
stands the disagreement is far larger. Any emulator can at best predict the expectation over such runs; this sets both
the target and the yardstick of the evaluation (Sect.~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

Four observations fix the architecture.

\emph{(i) The cost is the daily step and the patch ensemble} (Sect.~\ref{sec:target}). No process of the original
takes enough run time that accelerating it alone would give an order of magnitude, so a substantial speed-up must
replace the daily step as a whole, and it must predict the expectation over patches instead of simulating replicates.
Re-implementing physics can be faster than legacy code for a column model without a per-individual loop
\citep{R00975}; for an individual-tree model the per-tree daily step is the cost.

\emph{(ii) One-step learning of the forest state drifts when run freely.} A distributional random forest trained to
predict the number of trees per patch one year ahead reached an out-of-sample coefficient of determination of 0.982
--- but carrying the previous year's count forward already reached 0.962. Fed its own output, its area-weighted
warming response turned from +0.71 to $-0.23$ of the original's within decades, because it followed 96\,\% of large
increases but only 87\,\% of large decreases: a small, state-dependent asymmetry that accumulates. This is the failure
reported for one-step tree ensembles of ecLand \citep{R00067}.

\emph{(iii) One climate per place cannot teach a response.} Across the global grid, 76\,\% of a cell's warming is
predictable from its present climate, so with one historical and one future run the climate response is confounded
with geography --- the identification problem behind the out-of-distribution failures in the literature
\citep{R00289,R00430}. The training data must contrast many climates at the same place.

\emph{(iv) Water and carbon are exchanged through stores.} Conservation is reliable when the architecture enforces it
\citep{R00532,R00755}, and the stores must be prognostic for an atmosphere model to exchange water with them.

From these follow the design principles of LandEmu. \textbf{P1}: learn the whole daily step with one small network per
cell and day, keeping the water stores explicit and closed. \textbf{P2}: predict the expectation over the patch
ensemble, so that the number of patches drops out of the cost. \textbf{P3}: predict each year's forest state as a
departure from a per-cell anchor, driven by climate, rather than from last year's state, so that errors cannot
accumulate; carry from year to year only a bounded, weather-driven departure from that level (Sect.~\ref{sec:dep}).
\textbf{P4}: train on climate contrast --- many climate models and scenarios and several independent runs --- and test
on a climate model and a run never seen. \textbf{P5}: measure every error against the original's own run-to-run
variability.

\section{Emulator description}\label{sec:model}

LandEmu advances one year at a time in two blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}; Table~\ref{tab:io}). Block A, the daily
water--carbon model, runs 365 daily steps from the day's weather and last year's stand. Block B, the yearly forest
maps, runs once at the end of the year from climate statistics of the same weather and predicts the forest structure
and the stand, which becomes the daily model's input for the next year. Everything is driven by daily weather on the
0.5$^\circ$ grid: air temperature, precipitation, downward shortwave radiation and net longwave radiation; specific
humidity enters only the yearly climate statistics. All years have 365 days.

\subsection{Daily water--carbon model}\label{sec:daily}

\paragraph{Inputs.} For cell $c$ and day $t$ the network sees today's weather; trailing means of temperature (10 and
30 days) and shortwave radiation (30 days) and trailing sums of precipitation (30, 90 and 365 days), which stand in for
the memory of the deep soil without carrying it as a state; the day of year (as sine and cosine), the sine of latitude
and the day length; yesterday's snow $S_{t-1}$ and top-metre water $W_{t-1}$, $W_{t-1}$ divided by the capacity, and
the capacity itself; and the stand at the end of the previous year (leaf area index, vegetation carbon and the cover of
the ten plant types). No input is taken from the fluxes of the run being emulated. The top-metre water capacity is
recomputed by LPJmL-FIT from its evolving soil carbon; in the evaluation runs it is taken each year from the run being
emulated, in the released emulator it is fixed at each cell's 2019 value. Over the century it changes by a median
0.1--0.2\,\% (at most 4--6\,\% in single cells), so the difference is immaterial. The soil enters only through this
capacity: soil texture, which sets how fast water enters and moves through the soil, is not an input
(Sect.~\ref{sec:choices}).

\paragraph{Network.} Each of five ensemble members is a multilayer perceptron with three hidden layers of 256 units
and SiLU activations; all outputs share one trunk. The outputs are mapped to bounded rates: an interception fraction
$i\in[0,1]$, a snowfall fraction $f\in[0,1]$ and a melt fraction $m\in[0,1]$, non-negative transpiration $E_T$, soil
evaporation $E_S$ and runoff $R$, a signed exchange $Q$ between the top metre and the deep soil, non-negative GPP and
plant respiration $R_a$, so that $\mathrm{NPP} = \mathrm{GPP} - R_a$. The ensemble mean of the five members is used.

\paragraph{Closed water bucket.} With precipitation $P_t$, the stores are updated as
\begin{align}
  S_t &= S_{t-1}(1-m) + f P_t, \qquad E_{I,t} = i P_t, \\
  W_t &= W_{t-1} + P_t - (S_t - S_{t-1}) - E_{I,t} - E_{T,t} - E_{S,t} - R_t - Q_t, \\
  D_t &= \left(D_{t-1} + Q_t\right)\left(1 - 1/\tau\right) ,
\end{align}
where $D$ is a deep store that drains the amount $(D_{t-1}+Q_t)/\tau$ into runoff each day, with a time constant
$\tau = 365$\,days. Water above the top-metre capacity is added to runoff and a deficit below zero is taken from the
deep store. Hence, every day and to rounding precision,
\begin{equation}
  P_t = \Delta(S + W + D)_t + E_{I,t} + E_{T,t} + E_{S,t} + R^{\mathrm{tot}}_t ,
\end{equation}
with $R^{\mathrm{tot}}$ the total runoff. The bounds restrict nothing the original does: on eight million cell-days
interception and snow accumulation never exceeded precipitation and no flux turned negative. Because the deep store can
be drawn below zero, its drainage term, and with it total runoff, turns slightly negative on a few days (minimum
$-2.5$ to $-10$\,mm per day in the cells tested).

\paragraph{Training.} The networks are trained one step ahead (yesterday's stores from the original) on 40 million
sampled cell-days of 730 training cells, with 80 further cells for early stopping, minimising the sum over the eight
targets of the squared errors standardised by each target's spread (Adam with a one-cycle learning-rate schedule
peaking at $3\times10^{-3}$, batches of 16\,384, ten epochs; about 15 minutes per member on one GPU). They are then run
freely: the stores are carried by the model over 81 years. Specific humidity is not an input, because the original's
daily exchange does not read it.

\subsection{Yearly climate statistics}

At the end of each year, LandEmu computes 25 statistics of the year's daily weather: mean, coldest-month and
warmest-month temperature; degree days above 5\,$^\circ$C; frost days and days above 30\,$^\circ$C; annual and summer
precipitation; potential evaporation; the annual, summer and driest three-month climatic water balance; the longest
dry spell; mean relative humidity; summer vapour pressure deficit and its 10-day extremes; mean shortwave and net
longwave radiation; and, for each of the seven tree types, the number of days outside its temperature-stress range.
These enter a trailing 30-year window, whose means are the climate the forest maps see.

\subsection{Yearly forest maps}\label{sec:maps}

For each yearly quantity $q$ (six structure quantities and twelve stand quantities) a separate gradient-boosted tree
model \citep{Ke2017} predicts
\begin{equation}
  M^{q}_{c,y} = A^{q}_{c,y} + g_q\!\left(\bar{x}_{c,y},\; \bar{x}_{c,y} - \bar{x}^{A}_{c,y},\; A_{c,y},\;
  s^{0}_{c},\; \Delta s^{0}_{c},\; \phi_c,\lambda_c,\sigma_c\right),
\end{equation}
where $A^{q}_{c,y}$ is the \emph{anchor}: the original's mean of $q$ at cell $c$ in year $y$ over its training runs
and climates; $\bar{x}_{c,y}$ is the trailing 30-year climate and $\bar{x}^{A}_{c,y}$ the corresponding climate of the
anchor runs, so that their difference measures how far this climate departs from what the anchor represents;
$A_{c,y}$ collects all anchors of the cell; $s^{0}_{c}$ is the run's own 2000--2019 state and $\Delta s^{0}_{c}$ its
offset from the training runs' mean (the known start state); and $\phi$, $\lambda$, $\sigma$ are latitude, longitude
and soil code. Biomass per tree is predicted as a logarithmic ratio to its anchor, which weights relative errors
equally across dense and sparse stands; the other quantities as differences. Each model has 400 trees with 31 leaves,
learning rate 0.05 and 80\,\% row and column subsampling, and is trained on every fourth year of the training runs:
five runs under nine climate models in the test folds (about three million cell-years), all six runs under all ten
climate models in the deployment release (about four million).

Where the year-to-year development comes from matters for what the maps can and cannot do. In the panel version the
anchor is the original's own year-by-year trajectory at that cell, averaged over its training runs and the other
climate models --- that is, it includes the future output of the original at that cell, but never that of the run or
the climate model being predicted. The 30-year climate window moves slowly on top of it. Because no prediction is fed
back, a year's error cannot carry into the next (P3); but for the same reason a single extreme year moves the window by
only one thirtieth and leaves no legacy in the level. The stand is the only quantity passed from the maps to the daily
model; it affects next year's fluxes but not next year's maps. In the full-grid version (Sect.~\ref{sec:global}) and in
the nitrogen-on version (Sect.~\ref{sec:resp}) the anchor is instead each cell's own simulated historical state,
constant in time, so the climate alone carries time. Like any tree ensemble, the maps cannot extrapolate: their
outputs stay within the range of the training targets, which damps responses beyond the training climates
(Sects.~\ref{sec:resp} and~\ref{sec:global}).

\subsection{Weather-driven departure of tree density and biomass}\label{sec:dep}

A forest that loses a third of its trees in a drought year does not return to its expected level the next year. To let
the emulator carry such events, a second gradient-boosted model per quantity predicts, for tree density and biomass per
tree, the departure
\begin{equation}
  D^{q}_{c,y} = \ln\frac{X^{q}_{c,y}+1}{M^{q}_{c,y}+1},\qquad
  D^{q}_{c,y} = h_q\!\left(D^{n}_{c,y-1},\, D^{a}_{c,y-1},\, M^{n}_{c,y},\, M^{a}_{c,y},\, \bar{x}_{c,y},\,
  z_{c,y},\, z_{c,y-1}\right),
\end{equation}
where $X$ is the emulated value, $M$ the forest map's, $n$ and $a$ stand for tree density and biomass per tree, and
$z_{c,y}$ are the 25 yearly climate statistics of year $y$ as anomalies against the 30 years before (in standard
deviations). It is trained on the departures of the original's training runs from forest maps fitted without them
(cross-fitted in three folds of climate models), averaged over the five runs that share each climate --- the
weather-forced part --- and with each series' long-term mean removed, so that it changes the year-to-year course but
not the long-run level. It runs forward year by year from zero in the first emulated year. Carrying two numbers per
cell, it is the only recursive part of the forest maps; Sect.~\ref{sec:dieoff} shows what it does.

\subsection{Choice of architecture and settings}\label{sec:choices}

\emph{Forest maps and departure model.} Each setting of the gradient-boosted maps was varied on its own around the
release, refitted for all five held-out climate models and scored on the 15 held-out cases as the ratio of its error
to the release's (median over cases; Table~\ref{tab:choices_map}). An alternative would have replaced a release
setting had it reduced the error of both dense-stand tree density and biomass per tree by at least 2\,\% without
worsening any quantity by more than 2\,\%; none did. The release sits on a plateau: fewer leaves (15), fewer trees
(200) or a lower learning rate (0.025) each raise the dense-stand biomass-per-tree error by 2.6--3.0\,\%; more leaves
(63), more trees (800) or a higher rate (0.1) each lower the dense tree-density error by 1.4--3.1\,\% but not the
biomass error by 2\,\% (63 leaves: $-3.1$ and $-1.8$\,\%, and no quantity worse). Combining 63 leaves with 800 trees
lowers both errors by 3.0 and 4.8\,\% and the sparse-stand density error by 10\,\%, but the gain is largest in
2021--2040 (about 4\,\%) and fades to 1--3\,\% by 2081--2100, and with the departure model it shrinks to 1.4 and
3.9\,\%; at four times the model size it was not adopted. Without subsampling, or with at
least 100 rows per leaf, the errors change by less than 2.5\,\%. A 50-year climate window is worse (biomass per tree
+3.6\,\%), 10 and 20 years are equivalent to 30; training rows every second or eighth year instead of every fourth
are equivalent. Every input group is needed: without the climate offset from the anchor runs the biomass error rises
by 11\,\% and the sparse-stand density error by 14\,\%, and the late-century drift of biomass grows (1.56 instead of
1.41); without the anchors as inputs the errors rise by 1--2\,\%; without the run's own 2000--2019 state the density
and biomass errors are unchanged but the dense-stand trait medians are 13--41\,\% worse. For the departure model,
15 or 63 leaves, 200 or 800 trees and a 15- or 20-year anomaly window all changed the errors by less than 3\,\%;
the share of a die-off it reproduces moved between 0.15 and 0.21 (release 0.18).

Evidence already measured:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Daily network size.} A two-layer network of 64 units left a worst held-out-cell NPP error of 7.9\,\% and
        area totals off by more than 5\,\%; three layers of 256 units reduced it to 5.7\,\%, at 3.3 times the cost
        (1.3$\times10^{-3}$ instead of 4.0$\times10^{-4}$ core-seconds per cell-year and network).
  \item \emph{Ensemble size.} A single network's NPP level shifts by several per cent with its random seed, and one
        network's error is 1.01--1.28 (worst 1.72) times that of five; three networks lost 10--11\,\% on
        evapotranspiration and runoff in one of two replicates; ten networks improved evapotranspiration by 6--9\,\%
        and runoff by 6--13\,\% at twice the cost, which puts the emulator at the limit of the T31 allowance. The
        members' spread is not used as an uncertainty estimate.
  \item \emph{Training sample.} 80 million cell-days instead of 40 million changed NPP by less than run-to-run noise;
        160 million biased NPP area totals by $-9$ to $-10$\,\%.
  \item \emph{Deep store.} Without drainage the deep store accumulated 2--3\,m of water over 81 years and the runoff
        error was 11--14 times a further run's; with $\tau=365$\,days it is 3.9--5.5 times; 30 days gave the same
        30-year means and worse single years.
  \item \emph{NPP as GPP minus respiration} rather than a separate output: area totals of NPP off by 2.3 instead of
        5.4\,\%. A structured photosynthesis head with separable limiting factors fitted daily GPP 2.3 times worse.
  \item \emph{Soil texture} as four further inputs left evapotranspiration unchanged ($-1$\,\%), improved runoff by
        5--11\,\% and NPP by 13--16\,\% in two replicate pairs; the released version does not yet use it.
  \item \emph{The anchor.} Without an anchor the forest map agreed with the unseen run on fewer cells than a plain
        lookup of the training runs; with it, it beats the lookup in every case.
  \item \emph{Recursion.} Feeding the map its own last-year state remembered a start error for 10--20 years but let
        errors build up (error growth 1.55 for tree density and 1.86 for biomass per tree, against 1.22 and 1.41).
  \item \emph{Targets.} A logarithmic target for biomass per tree lowered its typical error from 1.42 to 1.26 times a
        further run; Poisson and Tweedie objectives for tree density only moved error between sparse and dense cells.
  \item \emph{Inputs.} Leaving out latitude, longitude and soil code changed the scores by less than 3\,\%; the soil
        code as a category or as texture values by less than 1\,\%. Plant-type cover fitted additively beats a
        relative or sum-constrained fit (dense tree cover 1.55 against 1.85 and 1.58 times a further run).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

LandEmu is a Python package whose only run-time dependencies are numpy and LightGBM; the networks are ported from
PyTorch to numpy (maximum difference $10^{-6}$ of each output's range on 20\,000 real input rows). The yearly climate
statistics and their anomalies equal those of the training pipeline exactly (to $4\times10^{-11}$ for the anomalies),
and a validation release reproduces the scored daily fluxes of the evaluation to rounding (median per-cell difference
of 30-year means 1.5--2.9$\times10^{-4}$) and the yearly quantities with a median difference of at most
$2\times10^{-4}$ of their typical size. The largest remaining yearly differences have one cause: the training table converted the forcing, stored in 0.1\,$^\circ$C steps, in single
precision, so days at exactly 30.0\,$^\circ$C counted as hot days; continuous forcing from a host model never sits
exactly on such a threshold. A release (models plus per-cell set-ups for 1\,050 cells) occupies about 190\,MB. The
interface offers a whole year (\texttt{step\_year}) or a day at a time (\texttt{begin\_year}, 365\,\x\,
\texttt{step\_day}, \texttt{end\_year}, bit-identical to the yearly call), so that a host model can exchange fluxes
daily.

What a deployment needs follows from the anchor: every emulated cell needs a historical simulation of LPJmL-FIT, which
supplies the anchors, the 2000--2019 state, the start stores and stand and the water capacity. In the panel version the
anchor also uses the training runs' future output at that cell (other runs and other climate models, never the run
being predicted); the full-grid and nitrogen-on versions use only the historical state.

\subsection{Variant with a \texorpdfstring{CO$_2$}{CO2} input}\label{sec:co2var}

For LPJmL-FIT with nitrogen limitation and rising CO$_2$, the same architecture receives the year's CO$_2$
concentration as an additional daily-network input, and the forest maps receive the trailing 30-year mean CO$_2$ and its
change since 1990--2019. It is trained on runs of that model (Sect.~\ref{sec:resp}). This variant and the full-grid
version use the forest maps without the departure model.

\section{Training data and evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Simulations of the original}

The main data set is a panel of 1\,050 cells: 105 blocks of ten contiguous 0.5$^\circ$ cells, chosen to balance 20
climate strata (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}a). Each block was spun up for 1000 years and run over 2000--2019 under observed
weather (GSWP3-W5E5; \citealt{Lange2021W5E5}) and over 2020--2100 under bias-adjusted output \citep{Lange2019} of ten
CMIP6 climate models (GFDL-ESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, UKESM1-0-LL, CanESM5, CNRM-CM6-1,
CNRM-ESM2-1, EC-Earth3, MIROC6) for three scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5; \citealt{ONeill2016}), plus a
control with observed 1990--2019 years resampled. We call each combination of climate model and scenario a \emph{leg}.
Six independent runs exist, each with its own spin-up and random seed, all with 25 patches, nitrogen limitation off and
CO$_2$ constant after 2019. Per-tree output is written every year (trees taller than 5\,m, as in the original's
standard output); daily fluxes are written for 13 legs of five climate models and for the control, in runs 1--4.

\subsection{Held-out tests}

The forest maps are tested in folds that hold out one of five climate models entirely, together with run 4: they are
trained on the other nine climate models and runs 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6, and run 4 under the held-out model is the truth
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}b). With three scenarios this gives 15 test cases. The cells themselves are seen in training, as
they would be in deployment (Sect.~\ref{sec:limits}). The daily networks are trained on runs 1--4 under all 14 legs
with daily output and tested on 24 blocks (240 cells, 238 with GPP of at least 50\,gC\,m$^{-2}$\,yr$^{-1}$) never used
in their training, including the five biome blocks of Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}. For them, therefore, only the place is
new: they have seen every climate model they are scored on. In the full emulator the daily networks read the stand
predicted by forest maps that never saw the test run or the test climate model.

\subsection{Measuring error against the original's own variability}

Because a second run of LPJmL-FIT differs from the first, a fixed tolerance such as ``within 10\,\%'' is uninformative:
two runs of the original agree on all six structure quantities within 10\,\% (or within the typical two-run spread of
the cell's density class, where that is larger) in only 17--21\,\% of cells. Following practice in climate-model
emulation and model verification \citep{WatsonParris2022,Baker2015,Lutjens2025}, we instead compare the distribution of
errors over cells with that of a further run. For cell $c$ and quantity $q$ against the unseen truth run $T$, the
emulator's error is $e_E(c)=|E(c)-T(c)|/|T(c)|$ and a further run $R$'s error is $e_R(c)=|R(c)-T(c)|/|T(c)|$, pooled
over the available further runs. The \emph{error ratio} at centile $k$ is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_k = \frac{P_k\left[e_E\right]}{P_k\left[e_R\right]},
\end{equation}
with $k=50$ (the typical cell) and $k=90$ (the worst tenth of cells). A further run of the original scores
$\rho\approx1$ by construction; the mean of three runs scores about 0.82, and a perfect predictor of the expectation
about 0.71, because an average beats a single random realisation. Values below one are therefore possible and, for a
model that predicts the expectation, expected. We apply the ratio to 30-year means (2071--2100 for structure;
2021--2050 and 2071--2100 for fluxes), to the climate response (late minus early period), and to single years (for
drift). Area totals (cosine-latitude weighted) are reported as signed relative errors, because a coherent bias of a few
per cent can hide behind small per-cell errors yet does not average out in a sum.

Forests with at least five trees per patch in the anchor are reported as \emph{dense} (about 80\,\% of tree-bearing
cells), the others as \emph{sparse}; classing them by the anchor rather than by the truth run avoids selecting cells on
the outcome being predicted. Each number is the median over the 15 test cases, with the range over cases where it
matters. \emph{Drift} means the gap to the original growing, not the values changing; we measure it as the ratio of the
single-year typical error ratio late in the century (2081--2100) to that of an earlier period. For the forest
structure we report two baselines: 2021--2040, when the known start state still makes the emulator's error smaller than
a further run's, and 2051--2070, after that knowledge has faded.

\paragraph{Reference arms.} Every score is reported beside (i) a further run scored as if it were the emulator, which
must give about 1 and checks the scorer; (ii) the mean of other runs, a reference for a near-ideal predictor of the
expectation; (iii) the anchor alone, without the learned departure; and (iv) the run's 2019 state carried forward,
which shows whether the measure can detect drift.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How the forest maps behave at a single cell}

Figure~\ref{fig:cell} shows what the yearly maps do. At the temperate and the boreal cell the original's runs scatter
widely: its unseen run and a further run of the same climate wander apart by up to two trees per patch and by 50\,\% in
biomass per tree for decades, because a few deaths or recruitments in 25 patches change the stand. The anchor --- the
mean of the training runs under other climates --- smooths this noise but knows nothing about the held-out climate. The
emulator follows the anchor where the climate resembles the training climates and departs from it where it does not:
at the temperate cell it keeps biomass per tree above the declining anchor in the second half of the century, as the
unseen run does. It does not reproduce the excursions of the single run, and it should not: they are not predictable
from climate.

\subsection{Forest structure}\label{sec:structure}

Over all 15 held-out cases (Fig.~\ref{fig:structure}), the typical error of tree density in dense forests is 1.22
times that of a further run (cases 1.12--1.45), and that of biomass per tree 1.18 times (1.03--1.61). The four trait
medians are closer to the unseen run than a further run is (0.59--0.86 times): the emulator predicts the expectation,
while a further run is itself a random draw. In the worst tenth of dense cells tree density reaches 1.40 times and
biomass per tree 1.13 times a further run. Area totals over dense cells are off by a median of $-2.6$\,\% for stems
(cases $-4.7$ to $-0.2$\,\%) and $-1.3$\,\% for biomass ($-8.6$ to $+2.7$\,\%). Sparse stands are harder: tree density
2.90 times a further run, biomass per tree 1.55 times, stem and biomass totals a median $-3.3$\,\% ($-14$ to $+12$\,\%)
and $-1.5$\,\% ($-19$ to $+8$\,\%). The departure model of Sect.~\ref{sec:dep} contributes to these numbers: the
forest maps alone give 1.26 and 1.18 for dense tree density and biomass per tree, 1.47 for tree density in the worst
tenth, 3.00 and 1.47 in sparse stands, and dense area totals of $-0.2$ and $-1.5$\,\%. So the departure model lowers
the tree-density errors by 3--5\,\% and makes sparse biomass per tree 5\,\% worse and the dense stem total 2.4 points
more negative. Classing dense cells by the held-out run instead of the anchor gives similar ratios (forest maps alone:
1.20 for tree density and 1.16 for biomass per tree in dense cells, 3.15 for sparse tree density).

\subsection{Drift of the forest state}\label{sec:drift}

Run year by year from 2020 to 2100 (Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}), the emulator's error starts below that of a further run,
because it knows the run's own 2000--2019 state, and rises over about three decades as that knowledge fades. It then
levels off: tree density at 1.06--1.24 times a further run's error in the 2050s and 1.07--1.41 in the 2090s (range over
cases), biomass per tree at 0.96--1.24 and 0.99--1.68. From 2051--2070 to 2081--2100 the typical error grows by a
median factor of 1.05 for both tree density (cases 0.99--1.14) and biomass per tree (0.97--1.36); the trait medians
grow by 1.11--1.20 while remaining at 0.63--0.88 times a further run. Measured from 2021--2040, when the start state
still keeps the error low, the growth is 1.20 for tree density and 1.41 for biomass per tree. The rise in the first
decades does not depend on the scenario (tree density 1.22 under SSP1-2.6, 1.23 under SSP5-8.5), which identifies it as
the fading start state rather than climate extrapolation. What remains is a slow creep of tree density in the second
half of the century that increases with warming (+0.05, +0.10 and +0.12 from the 2050s to the 2090s for SSP1-2.6,
SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5). The reference arms behave as they should: a further run stays at 1.0, the mean of other runs at
0.8, the anchor alone rises steadily to 1.3--1.6, and the carried-forward 2019 state rises to more than twice a further
run's error, so the measure does detect drift.

\subsection{Drought die-offs}\label{sec:dieoff}

Year by year, the original's forests do not change smoothly. Over the 15 test cases, 3\,483 dense cells of the
unseen run lose more than 15\,\% of their trees in a single year of 2025--2090 (counting each cell's largest loss), and
in 94\,\% of these events at least two of the three further runs lose at least half as much in the same year; on
average they reproduce 94\,\% of the loss. The losses are forced by the weather, not by chance. They are droughts: in the event year, annual precipitation is a median 1.25 standard deviations below the
cell's normal, the climatic water balance 1.20 and relative humidity 1.18; the year before is dry too. Over the 3\,291
such events of the 15 test cases, the original loses a median 33\,\% of its trees in the event year and is still
15\,\% below its earlier level ten years later (Fig.~\ref{fig:dieoff}).

The forest maps alone, whose climate input is a trailing 30-year mean, follow 3\,\% of such a drop. With the
departure model the emulator follows 18\,\%, and its error in the ten years after an event falls from 4.5 to 3.8 times
that of a further run (stand biomass: 7 to 16\,\% of the drop). The same model without the weather inputs follows no more than the maps alone (3\,\%), so the gain comes from
the weather anomaly.

What limits it is the mechanism of the die-offs. Reconstructing each tree's leaf carbon from the original's per-tree
output shows that 94--98\,\% of the trees removed in these years had less leaf carbon than a sapling of their own
specific leaf area --- the threshold below which LPJmL-FIT removes a tree --- and sat at a median 0.58 of it, while no
tree that died by the random mortality draw did. The deaths in event years equal those expected from the trees' own
mortality probabilities (34.2 against 34.3\,\%), so fire, which is active in these runs, does not cause them. A
die-off is therefore many trees crossing an individual threshold in the same year, driven by a one-year collapse of
their carbon balance. Neither the weather nor the stand describes this well: the original's own yearly NPP anomaly
separates event years from other years with an area under the receiver-operating curve of 0.90, a forcing-only daily
soil-water index 0.88, and the share of trees close to the threshold the year before 0.49--0.57. Adding the daily
soil-water index and runs of consecutive dry years to the departure model raised the share of the drop it follows only
to 25\,\%, at the price of worse sparse-stand biomass.

\subsection{Daily carbon and water exchange}\label{sec:fluxes}

At the three biome cells of Fig.~\ref{fig:daily} the full emulator --- daily networks never trained on these cells,
fed a stand from forest maps that never saw the climate model or the run --- reproduces the seasonal course and the
day-to-day variability of GPP and evapotranspiration: the spring onset in the temperate and boreal forests, the short
wet season at the semi-arid cell, the response to individual dry spells. Year-to-year variations of annual GPP follow
the original's at the temperate and semi-arid cells (correlation 0.93 and 0.94; a further run: 0.91 and 0.96), less so
at the boreal cell (0.65; a further run: 0.99). The 2021--2100 means differ by +0.8\,\% at the temperate and
$-0.6$\,\% at the boreal cell, but by +12\,\% at the semi-arid cell, where the original's own two runs differ by
3\,\%. The top-metre water store is the least faithful daily variable: at the temperate cell it dries 20\,mm too deep
in summer, and at the boreal cell it holds up to 50\,mm more water in summer than the original, while the fluxes stay
close. The networks are trained on fluxes and store changes, and the store is a state integrated by the bucket, not a
prediction target.

Over the 238 held-out cells and 13 legs (Fig.~\ref{fig:dailyscores}), the typical error of 30-year mean GPP is 2.4
times a further run's (median over legs), evapotranspiration 3.1 times, NPP 3.9 times and runoff 5.2 times. Since two
runs of the original differ by only 1.0--1.6\,\% in a cell's 30-year GPP, evapotranspiration or NPP, this means typical
per-cell errors of about 3--6\,\% for GPP and evapotranspiration and 5--6\,\% for NPP. Area totals are off by a median
of 1\,\% for GPP, NPP and evapotranspiration and 2\,\% for runoff, and by at most 2.9\,\% on any leg. The climate
response (2071--2100 minus 2021--2050) is 1.8--2.4 times a further run's error. Runoff is hardest because it is a small
residual of precipitation minus evapotranspiration, so the evapotranspiration error passes into it amplified; NPP is
hard because it is a small difference of two large terms in the most productive cells.

The emulated stand costs accuracy. Fed the original's own stand instead, the same daily networks reach 2.2 (GPP), 2.6
(evapotranspiration), 3.4 (NPP) and 4.5 (runoff) times a further run's error. Compared leg by leg and period by period,
the emulated stand adds a median 12\,\% to the GPP error and 37\,\% to the evapotranspiration error, most of it through
the cover of the plant types. On these legs the gap to the original does not grow for the median of any flux: the
late-to-early ratio of single-year errors is 0.90--1.04; it grows only for GPP on the two warmest legs (UKESM1-0-LL
SSP3-7.0, IPSL-CM6A-LR SSP5-8.5; 1.26 and 1.28). The deep water store stays within the range of the original's
change, and over 81 years the across-cell mean of the top-metre store stays between 80 and 91\,mm.

In this evaluation the climate models are seen by the daily networks; only the cells are new. That matters: in tests
with a climate model withheld from daily-network training entirely, the gap to the original in GPP and NPP grew by a
factor of 1.3--1.4 over the century. The cause was the daily carbon response degrading as the climate moved away from
the training climates, not the water stores, since a run with the stores reset daily from the original drifted
identically. Adding further climate models to training did not reduce this growth beyond run-to-run noise for
UKESM1-0-LL; it falls (to 1.17 for NPP) only when that model itself is in training.

  \label{fig:dailyscores}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Learning the warming response}\label{sec:resp}

The warming response is the property an ESM needs most and the one most emulators never test. We tested it on
LPJmL-FIT with nitrogen limitation and rising CO$_2$, run on 674 cells that each represent one region of a 674-region
climate classification of the land surface (25 patches each), under the ten climate models and three scenarios, with
CO$_2$-fixed twins of every scenario for the training climate models. Two climate models of low and high climate
sensitivity, MPI-ESM1-2-HR and IPSL-CM6A-LR, were held out entirely, together with 20\,\% of the cells; both lie
within the warming range of the eight training models, of which CanESM5 and UKESM1-0-LL warm more, so the test climate
is covered by the training climates. An independent second run of the original exists for the held-out legs. Here the
forest maps are anchored on each cell's own 1990--2019 state, so no future output of the original enters the
prediction.

With rising CO$_2$, the original's vegetation carbon increases by 32--45\,\% by 2071--2100 on the held-out legs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:resp}a), mostly through CO$_2$ fertilisation. The emulator reproduces 81--97\,\% of this change, while
the original's independent second run reproduces 61--89\,\%: the original's own global response varies by up to
40\,\% between runs on this grid, because single cells stand for whole regions. Trained only on SSP1-2.6, the same
maps reproduce 37--43\,\% --- the failure seen when an emulator meets a climate warmer than its training climates.

With CO$_2$ fixed at its 2019 level, the original loses vegetation carbon under warming, by up to 20.7\,\% for
IPSL-CM6A-LR under SSP5-8.5 (second run: 26.3\,\%). The emulator reproduces 72\,\% of this loss and of the
IPSL-CM6A-LR loss under SSP3-7.0. The CO$_2$-fixed twins of SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 were added to training after the
first held-out results, in which twins of SSP3-7.0 alone gave 67 and 57\,\% of these losses; the held-out models and
cells were unchanged and nothing else was tuned. Contrasting warming with and without CO$_2$ rise at strong warming is
therefore what teaches the warming-only response. Without such twins the maps predict carbon gains on all four warming-only legs, because all their training
legs carry rising CO$_2$. Per cell (Fig.~\ref{fig:resp}b) the emulator's change is as close to the original's as the
second run's is (root-mean-square error 0.65--0.80 times the second run's), and the slopes of both against the
original are below one (0.73 and 0.75): the original's per-cell change contains run-to-run noise, which flattens the
regression slope of any predictor, including a perfect estimate of the expected response.

For GPP the picture splits. The daily networks fed the original's stand reproduce the global GPP change within 4\,\%
and the per-cell change with slopes of 0.92--0.99, with rising and with fixed CO$_2$. Fed the emulated stand, the
global change with rising CO$_2$ is still within 6\,\%, but the per-cell slope falls to 0.66--0.79, because the forest
maps' response of leaf area and tree cover is too uniform across cells (slopes 0.73--0.83, against 0.89--0.96 for the
second run). The global totals are right; the cell-to-cell spread of the response is too small. On the held-out cells,
a learning curve over 135, 269 and 539 training cells reduced this error by 6--12\,\% per doubling, which points to the
number of training cells rather than the model form as the limit.

\subsection{Full-grid application}\label{sec:global}

To show that the emulator runs globally, we retrained the forest maps on four runs of LPJmL-FIT (nitrogen off) under
GFDL-ESM4 --- historical, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 --- made with a different build of the model, anchored each cell
on its own simulated 1985--2014 state, and emulated each future scenario with maps that never saw it. The daily
networks were not retrained (these runs store no daily output). LandEmu then ran on all 64\,240 land cells from 1975
to 2100 (Fig.~\ref{fig:global}). Over 1985--2014 the emulated global GPP equals the original's (+0.04\,\%), NPP is
1.1\,\% high, transpiration 8.5\,\% low and runoff 5.8\,\% high. Benchmarked against observations with the ILAMB
system, the emulator's overall scores are within 0.03 of the original's on all 27 data-set comparisons; for biomass,
leaf area and cover this is not a test of the emulator, because its historical forest state is anchored on the
original's. Over the same period the original's GPP rises from 151 to 162\,PgC\,yr$^{-1}$ because CO$_2$ rises in its
historical forcing, while the emulator's stays flat: the nitrogen-off emulator has no CO$_2$ input by design
(Sect.~\ref{sec:target}), so it reproduces the level but not this trend.

Under the unseen scenarios, the typical per-cell error in 2071--2100 is 6.0--6.4\,\% for GPP, 4.4--4.8\,\% for
evapotranspiration, 11--15\,\% for vegetation carbon and 7--8\,\% for dense-cell tree density, always smaller than when
the 1985--2014 state is carried forward (11--15\,\%, 7--12\,\%, 18--26\,\% and 10--11\,\%). The global responses agree in
sign in 13 of 15 cases (five quantities, three scenarios), but the carbon responses are 3--5 percentage points too low,
and under SSP3-7.0, the warmest scenario and the only one outside the training range, the emulator captures only a
third of the original's vegetation-carbon loss ($-2.8$ against $-9.2$\,\%). This is the extrapolation limit of
Sect.~\ref{sec:resp} reproduced on the full grid: with only milder scenarios in training, the strongest response is
damped. On an earlier set of eight independent runs of this model under GFDL-ESM4, scored over all 57\,726 tree-bearing
cells against a further run, an earlier form of the forest maps that predicts 30-year windows rather than single years
reaches 0.92 times a further run's error for dense tree density and biomass per tree when only the run is held out, and
1.24 and 1.47 times when the scenario is held out as well.

\subsection{Cost}\label{sec:cost}

Measured end to end on one core over 1\,050 cells and 81 years, the whole emulator costs $7.0\times10^{-3}$
core-seconds per cell-year (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed}), 97\,\% of it in the daily networks. LPJmL-FIT, timed on one core of
the same computing cluster at the temperate cell (marginal rate), costs 0.267 core-seconds at 25 patches and 7.06 at
500 patches, so the emulator needs 38 times fewer core-seconds than the original at its training setting and about
1\,000 times fewer than at the publication setting. The original's cost depends on how many trees a cell carries: across the
five biome cells it is 0.20--0.33 core-seconds at 25 patches, which gives factors of 29--47. The emulator lies inside
the allowance for an intermediate-resolution atmosphere by a factor of 1.9 (T31) to 4.3 (T63). The nitrogen-on variant
costs $7.3\times10^{-3}$ core-seconds, 115--130 times less than that model on the same cells. Training is cheap as
well: about 15 GPU-minutes per network and seconds to minutes per forest map. The cost is dominated by one matrix
product per day and network, so batching over years or a GPU would reduce it further; neither is used here.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{What the design buys}

Three design choices carry most of the result. \emph{Learning the whole daily step} is what makes the emulator fast:
the large speed-ups in the literature come from replacing an expensive iterative solver or a whole model step
\citep{R00067,R02497,R00568}, and the profile of LPJmL-FIT shows that nothing short of this can give an order of
magnitude for an individual-tree model. \emph{Predicting the expectation} removes the patch ensemble, which is a
variance-reduction device of the original, not a process; at the publication setting it is worth more than every
process combined. \emph{Keeping the forest level non-recursive} sidesteps the error accumulation that made a one-step
model of tree counts unusable and that rollout losses \citep{R00593,R01133} or trajectory fitting \citep{R00661}
address in other emulators; the only recursive part of the forest maps is a bounded, weather-driven departure from
that level. The daily stores are recursive too, but they are closed physical stores rather than a learned latent
state, and they did not walk away from the original in 81-year free runs. Table~\ref{tab:req} sets LandEmu against
the properties an ESM land component needs (Sect.~\ref{sec:intro}).

\subsection{What limits the fidelity}

Four findings locate the remaining error. First, the emulator is \emph{data-limited in the space of climates, not in
the number of runs}. On the panel, adding five climate models to training raised the share of cells close to the
unseen run by 0.023 and lowered the biomass-per-tree error ratio from 1.64 to 1.42, while adding two runs of the
original raised it by only 0.005. The warming-response test makes the same point from the other side: with training
climates that cover the test climate the response is reproduced, with only milder climates it is damped by more than
half. Second, \emph{sparse stands} carry most of the structure error. Reweighting the loss towards them only moved error
between sparse and dense cells, while the mean of three runs is 0.82 times a further run in every density class --- so
their response is predictable in principle, and what is missing is information about how sparse cells respond. Third,
\emph{the year-to-year course is a property of individual trees}: the original's drought die-offs are threshold kills
of single trees, decided by each tree's leaf carbon in a year of carbon collapse, which no stand-level quantity
singles out sharply. Fourth, the daily networks' own error, two to three times a further run, still exceeds the
10--40\,\% that the emulated stand adds, but the stand's share is concentrated in evapotranspiration and in the
cell-to-cell spread of the climate response.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The original must have been run on every emulated cell.} The forest maps depart from a per-cell anchor,
        so a cell never simulated by LPJmL-FIT cannot be emulated. On the response test, cells held out of training
        reach 1.8--2.2 times a second run's error for leaf area and cover, against near-parity on training cells; leaving
        out latitude, longitude and soil code changed this by less than 5\,\%, so the maps do not rely on recognising
        places, and the error fell by 6--12\,\% per doubling of the training cells. Unseen places are a matter of
        training-set size.
  \item \textbf{The structure tests hold out the climate model and the run, not the place}, as in deployment on the
        cells the original was run on.
  \item \textbf{The daily networks have seen every climate model they were scored on.} With a climate model withheld,
        their GPP and NPP gap to the original grew 1.3--1.4-fold over the century. NPP and runoff are the least
        accurate fluxes (3.9 and 5.2 times a further run's error), and the climate response of every flux is 1.8--2.4
        times a further run's error.
  \item \textbf{Soil.} The daily networks see the soil only through its top-metre water capacity, not its texture; how
        fast water moves through the soil, which matters most for runoff, is therefore learned only implicitly.
  \item \textbf{Extrapolation.} Gradient-boosted trees cannot predict outside the range of their training targets, and
        under a scenario warmer than all training scenarios the response is damped (Sect.~\ref{sec:global});
        deployment should keep the training climates broader than the target climate.
  \item \textbf{Die-offs.} The emulator follows 18\,\% of the original's drought die-offs.
  \item \textbf{Scope of the outputs.} The structure targets cover trees taller than 5\,m, as the original's standard
        output does, and the traits are represented by their medians, not their full distributions. There are no
        energy fluxes and no net CO$_2$ flux.
  \item \textbf{Scale of the evaluation.} The detailed evaluation uses 1\,050 of 54\,020 tree-bearing cells; the
        full-grid application has one run per scenario, so its errors cannot be set against a further run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Outlook}

The day-by-day interface makes LandEmu callable from an atmosphere model; experiments with Earth system models will be
reported separately. The most direct improvements are training on a full-grid set of runs that covers warmer climates
(which removes the unseen-cell limit and the extrapolation problem together), soil texture as a daily input, explicit
litter and soil carbon pools so that the net CO$_2$ flux is complete, and a calibrated predictive spread scored against
several independent runs of the original. Following the original's die-offs further needs a representation of how the
year's carbon balance moves the leaf carbon of groups of trees against their threshold, for example a small cohort
model driven by the emulated NPP.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:concl}

LandEmu emulates an individual-tree, trait-based DGVM as a whole: its daily carbon and water exchange and its yearly
forest structure, trait composition and stand, from daily weather alone, with water conserved exactly. Its design ---
learn the whole daily step, predict the expectation over the patch ensemble, predict the forest level from climate
rather than from last year's state, train on climate contrast and judge against the original's own variability ---
follows from where the original spends its run time and from what the literature shows to fail. Under a held-out
climate model and run, dense forests are reproduced close to the original's run-to-run variability (tree density 1.22,
biomass per tree 1.18 times a further run's error; trait medians better than a further run), and the structure error
grows only slowly once the start state has faded. The original's drought die-offs are threshold kills of individual
trees, of which the emulator follows a fifth. On places held out from their training, the daily networks reproduce GPP
and evapotranspiration to a few per cent per cell; under an unseen climate model their carbon fluxes drift. Trained on
climates that cover the test climate, the emulator reproduces the change in vegetation carbon under rising CO$_2$ as
well as a second run of the original does on the global total, but it damps the largest warming-only losses, the
cell-to-cell spread of the response and the response of sparse stands. At $7.0\times10^{-3}$ core-seconds per
cell-year it costs 38--1\,000 times less than the original and fits the land budget of an intermediate-resolution Earth
system model.
